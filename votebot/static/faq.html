Frequently asked questions

How VoteBot finds your ballot and the dates to vote, where its information comes from, how the money figures are put together, and where your picks are kept.

Finding your ballot

Which addresses and elections does VoteBot cover?

Texas addresses, for now. The list of elections comes from the Texas Secretary of State (Texas SOS). VoteBot shows the next one unless you choose another.

How does VoteBot know what's on my ballot?

First it finds your county and districts from your address. The US Census geocoder gives your U.S. House, State Senate and State House districts, and the Texas Legislative Council's maps give your State Board of Education district and your election precinct.

Then it takes the official ballot order for your county from the Texas Secretary of State and keeps the races for your districts. If Ballotpedia is on, it adds city council, school board and special district races, and any other race the state doesn't list, such as an appraisal district's board (these say "Listed by Ballotpedia"). It also adds Ballotpedia's notes on a race, such as a replacement nominee, and tells VoteBot your commissioner and JP precincts. With the Texas Secretary of State turned off in Settings, the ballot comes from Ballotpedia alone.

Why does VoteBot suggest addresses as I type?

To save typing and catch misspellings. Once you've typed a house number and a few letters of the street, VoteBot asks Ballotpedia's address search for matching Texas addresses. Picking one only fills in the box: press "Show my ballot" as usual.

What you type is sent to Ballotpedia through the VoteBot server, with "tx" added so the search looks in Texas. The server keeps a copy so the same text isn't sent twice. To stop that, turn off "Address suggestions" in Settings; your browser's own address autofill then works as before. Privacy has the details.

Is this my official ballot?

No. VoteBot is an unofficial helper, and it can be wrong or miss something, for example when a source hasn't caught up with a late change. Check the official sample ballot from your county elections office before you vote.

Why do I have to choose a party?

In a primary, each party has its own ballot. Texas voters don't register with a party: you choose which party's primary to vote in when you vote. Choose the ballot you plan to ask for.

If you vote in one party's primary, you can only vote in that party's runoff.

What does "Depends on your commissioner or JP precinct" mean?

County commissioner, justice of the peace and constable races are only on some ballots in a county, depending on your commissioner and justice of the peace precincts, and the Census doesn't say which ones you're in. In seven counties, VoteBot gets them from the county's own records (see "How does VoteBot know my commissioner and JP precincts?"); elsewhere, it uses Ballotpedia's answer when it has one. Ballotpedia only names a precinct that has a race on this ballot, so when yours has none this time, those races stay listed until you enter your precincts.

Until VoteBot knows them, those races are listed under "Depends on your commissioner or JP precinct". Those numbers are printed on your voter registration certificate; enter them under "Your districts" at the top of your ballot to narrow the list. You only need two numbers: your commissioner precinct, and your justice of the peace precinct, which also elects your constable.

The small print under "Your districts" says where each number came from: your county, Ballotpedia, or you. If one is wrong, the pencil at the top of "Your districts" turns every number, your U.S. House, State Senate, State House and SBOE districts too, into a box you can change or empty, and the reset arrow puts the looked-up ones back. Only the numbers you change are kept as yours; the others keep following your address, your county's records and Ballotpedia.

Where does my election precinct come from, and why might it be wrong?

Your election precinct is the "Pct" on your voter registration certificate: the small area your county groups voters into. VoteBot shows it under "Your districts" as "Precinct 300", and draws it on the map of your districts. It comes from the Texas Legislative Council, which collects every county's precincts after each statewide election and publishes them as one map.

VoteBot uses the Council's newest map, which the small print names (now "2026 Primary Election Voting Precincts"). A general election's map only comes out after that election, but precincts rarely change in between: counties can only redraw them in March or April of odd-numbered years, and 99.5% of precincts were the same in the 2022 primary and general, 99.9% in 2024. VoteBot checks for a newer map at most once a week, and downloads it when there is one. The map is about 45 MB, so the very first lookup takes longer.

It can still be wrong. A county can change a precinct before the Council's next map, and an address right by a precinct line can be placed on the wrong side of it, since the address lookup can place a house several feet off. VoteBot checks your address's point against the middle of its census block, and when they fall in different precincts it names both rather than guess, but it can't catch every such case. It shows no precinct for an address it could only place approximately. Your voter registration certificate, or My Voter Portal ("Am I registered?"), always wins.

VoteBot sends the Council nothing about you: it downloads the whole map and finds your precinct itself. Turn "Election precincts" off in Settings to leave it out.

Is my election precinct the same as my commissioner or JP precinct?

No. Your election precinct, the "Pct" on your certificate, is a small area, usually a few neighborhoods. Commissioner and justice of the peace precincts are much larger: every county has four commissioner precincts and a handful of JP precincts, and they decide which commissioner, JP and constable races are on your ballot.

Each election precinct lies inside one commissioner precinct and one JP precinct, so in seven counties VoteBot fills those in from your election precinct (see the next question). Elsewhere, enter them from the same certificate under "Your districts" (see "Depends on your commissioner or JP precinct").

How does VoteBot know my commissioner and JP precincts?

Texas law keeps each election precinct inside one commissioner precinct and one justice of the peace precinct, so once VoteBot knows your election precinct (see "Where does my election precinct come from"), your county's records say the other two. There's no statewide list, so this works in the counties whose records VoteBot reads:

  • Harris, Dallas, Tarrant, Travis and Fort Bend publish a list of their election precincts, with each one's commissioner and JP precinct. Fort Bend's has commissioner precincts only, so your JP precinct there comes from Ballotpedia, when your JP precinct has a race this time, or from you. VoteBot only uses a county's list when it has exactly the precincts on the Texas Legislative Council's map, so a list for another year, with precincts drawn differently, is never used.
  • Bexar and Denton publish maps of their commissioner and JP precincts instead. VoteBot takes points spread through your election precinct, each at least 160 feet from its edges, and gives a number only when they all fall in the same commissioner (or JP) precinct on the county's map. The two maps are drawn separately, so near a precinct's edge they can disagree by several feet; keeping 160 feet in gave no wrong numbers when checked against the lists of Dallas and Harris counties, for all 2,015 of their precincts. When your election precinct crosses a line on the county's map, or is too small to check, VoteBot leaves the number for you to enter, and a note says why.

Near the line between two election precincts, VoteBot gives a number only when both precincts have the same one. When the county's records and Ballotpedia disagree, VoteBot uses the county's and a note says so. A number you enter always wins.

VoteBot downloads a county's whole list or maps, at most once a week, when you look up an address there; your address is never sent. The counties publish proposals for new precincts too, which VoteBot never uses. Your voter registration certificate wins if they differ. Turn "Commissioner & JP precincts (counties)" off in Settings to leave them out.

What does the map of my districts show?

The map under "Your districts" is a street map with the outline of your U.S. House, State Senate, State House and State Board of Education districts and of your election precinct, and a pin at your address, which the map opens centered on, close enough to see your precinct's streets. Pick a district in the row of buttons above the map, or its line on the map, to highlight it and zoom to it; pick it again, or the pin button on the map, to come back to your address. Each district has its own color and its own kind of line: solid, dashed, dotted, or dashes and dots.

Drag the map to move it, and zoom with + and −, or with the scroll wheel once you've clicked the map, so scrolling the page never zooms it by accident. On a phone, use two fingers to move or zoom it; one finger scrolls the page.

The U.S. House and legislative outlines come from the US Census's TIGERweb, the same maps the Census uses to find your districts. VoteBot sends it only each district's number, never your address. The State Board of Education outline comes from the Texas Legislative Council's map, which VoteBot already keeps. Your election precinct's outline comes from the Council's precinct map, also already kept. The outlines are simplified to about 160 feet, your precinct's to about 16, so if you live near a boundary, go by the numbers, which come from the full maps.

The street map comes from OpenStreetMap. The VoteBot server asks for the map tiles of the area you look at, which shows OpenStreetMap roughly where your address is, and keeps them for a week, so looking again asks nothing. Turn "Street map" off in Settings to see the outlines alone, or "District map" off to leave out the Census's outlines.

Clicking the map's heading folds it away, as with a race, until you click it again; this browser remembers which you chose. While the map is folded, nothing is fetched for it.

Why are some races under "Special districts" or "Couldn't confirm"?

Ballotpedia lists water and municipal utility districts (MUDs) for your area, but you only vote in one if your home is inside it, and VoteBot can't tell whether it is. "Couldn't confirm" holds races for districts VoteBot couldn't place your address in. For both, check your county's sample ballot.

My address says it was placed "approximately". What does that mean?

When the Census can't match an address, VoteBot asks OpenStreetMap instead, which sometimes finds only the street or the area. The districts it shows may then not be yours, and VoteBot doesn't show your election precinct. Try the address exactly as it appears on your voter registration certificate, and double-check the districts.

Can I write in a candidate?

Yes. In Texas, a write-in vote only counts for someone who filed as a write-in candidate. The ones who filed with the Texas Secretary of State are listed in their race, after the printed names and marked "Write-in"; pick one and your printed sheet says "Name (write-in)", since you write the name in yourself. Every race also ends with a write-in line: type a name and it becomes your pick.

When and where to vote

Where do the dates under "When to vote" come from?

From the Texas Secretary of State's Important Election Dates page, which lists each election's last day to register, early voting and mail-ballot deadline. VoteBot shows the dates of the election on your ballot, and checks the page again once a day. The next date still to come is in bold; past ones are dimmed.

Early voting's places and hours are set by each county, so check with your county elections office. On Election Day, polls are open 7 a.m. to 7 p.m.

How do I add the dates to my calendar?

Click the calendar icon next to a date to download that date as a calendar file (.ics), or "Add all to calendar" for every date still to come; then open the file with your calendar app. Each date is an all-day event, with no place, since VoteBot doesn't know your polling place. If you add a date twice, your calendar updates the event rather than adding a copy.

"Add all" leaves out the mail-ballot deadline, since most voters can't vote by mail; its own icon is under "Voting by mail?".

Why doesn't VoteBot show my polling place?

Texas has no public way to look up a polling place by address. The state's My Voter Portal shows yours, but only after you log in with your name, date of birth and county, which VoteBot won't ask for. "Am I registered?" opens the portal, and "Where to vote" links to the list of county elections offices, which publish their polling places before each election. During early voting you can vote at any early-voting place in your county, and many counties let you do the same on Election Day.

Can I vote by mail?

In Texas, only if you're 65 or older, sick or disabled, away from your county for all of early voting and Election Day, expecting to give birth around Election Day, or in jail but still eligible to vote. Everyone else votes in person, early or on Election Day, which is why "Voting by mail?" starts closed.

Your application must reach your county's early voting clerk by the deadline shown there: received, not postmarked. The Texas Secretary of State explains how to apply.

Candidates and sources

Where does the information about candidates come from?

From the Texas Secretary of State's candidate filings, Ballotpedia, TrackAIPAC, and the campaign finance reports candidates file with the FEC or the Texas Ethics Commission (Campaign money below explains those). A candidate's badges and Details show what each source says, one tab per source, so you can see which source said what. The "Web search" link searches for the candidate's name, office and place.

Sources write names differently, so VoteBot matches them by name, with the seat and party to confirm. A ? means a source only likely matched the candidate, for example when only the first initial agrees ("Tom" and "Thomas"), so check it. "Incumbent" comes from the state's filing, or from Ballotpedia when it matches the candidate exactly in the same race.

Does VoteBot recommend candidates?

No. It puts what its sources say side by side. Your picks and notes are yours alone, and they stay in your browser.

What's the difference between "Matched" and "Likely match"?

Sources spell names differently, so VoteBot matches a candidate across sources by name and uses the seat and party to confirm it. "Matched" means the source's entry is this candidate. "Likely match" means it's probably the same person but not certain, so check it. A "?" marks it: on the source's tab in Details, and on the candidate's row, on that source's first badge (or on the Details button, if the source has no badge). When a match is ambiguous, VoteBot leaves it out rather than guess.

What is TrackAIPAC, and why does it show members of Congress as "likely"?

TrackAIPAC tracks money and endorsements from pro-Israel lobby groups for congressional candidates.

It lists members of Congress by the seat they hold now. Texas redrew its congressional districts in 2025, so someone running for a different seat in 2026 shows as a likely match unless TrackAIPAC's entry mentions the new seat.

How up to date is the information?

VoteBot keeps a copy of what each source sends back and asks again once it's old: after 24 hours for elections, key dates, candidates, Ballotpedia and polls, after 7 days for the FEC, and after 30 days for address lookups and address suggestions. If a source doesn't answer, or answers with an error, you'll see its last copy with a "data as of" note. Address suggestions, and addresses that weren't found, are no use as an old copy, so VoteBot deletes them the first time it starts once they've been expired for 30 days.

TrackAIPAC's and the Texas Ethics Commission's data come with VoteBot and are only fetched again when you press Refresh; Settings shows how old each one is. Campaigns file on a schedule, so the money figures move in steps: quarterly and just before the election for Congress, every six months and 30 and 8 days before an election for state offices.

To fetch something again right away, use Refresh on that source in Settings.

Why is a source paused?

When a source turns VoteBot away, VoteBot stops asking it for an hour rather than keep trying: Ballotpedia (its ballot and its address search, each on its own), FiftyPlusOne (polls), the Texas Secretary of State's dates page, the Census's TIGERweb (the district map), OpenStreetMap's tile server (the street map), the Texas Legislative Council's portal (the precinct and SBOE maps) and the counties' map servers (the commissioner and JP precincts) when they refuse a request, and the FEC when VoteBot reaches its rate limit. Settings shows which source is paused and until when.

Meanwhile, whatever the source already sent still shows. Addresses Ballotpedia already answered for still show its races, and other ballots are built from the other sources. With the FEC's shared demonstration key the limit comes quickly; see why the FEC tab shows only totals.

Campaign money

Where do the money figures come from?

From the reports campaigns must file. Candidates for Congress report to the FEC (Federal Election Commission). Candidates for statewide office, the Legislature, the State Board of Education, the appeals and district courts, and district attorney report to the Texas Ethics Commission (TEC).

"Raised" is what the candidate's campaign reported receiving. For Congress it covers the whole election period: two years for the House, six for the Senate. For state offices it counts the reports since the last even-year November general election, and the date is shown next to the figures. "Cash on hand" is from the candidate's latest report, and the date shown is when that report ends, not when VoteBot fetched it.

A candidate's details break the money down: small and large donations, individuals and political action committees (PACs), where donors live, and the largest donors. The next two answers say exactly how VoteBot puts the figures together, for the FEC and for the Texas Ethics Commission.

How are the FEC figures put together?

VoteBot asks the FEC's public API, OpenFEC, which serves the reports campaigns file with the FEC. Every figure is the FEC's own; VoteBot only picks them and groups them as described here.

Which campaigns

  • Your U.S. House district comes from the Census geocoder. The Senate race is statewide.
  • The FEC counts money in two-year periods named for their even year: 2026 covers 2025–26. VoteBot asks for the whole election, which for a Senate seat means the six years since that seat was last up.
  • One request per race lists everyone who filed for that seat, with their totals, including people who lost the primary. VoteBot matches them to the candidates on your ballot by name, with party and seat to confirm (see "Matched" and "Likely match"). A campaign the FEC lists under two candidate IDs is counted once.
  • With a free FEC key, each candidate on your ballot takes five more requests for the breakdowns below. Without one, only the totals show.

What each figure is

Raised
Everything the campaign reported receiving: donations, the candidate's own money and loans, transfers from other committees, refunds and interest.
Spent
Everything the campaign reported paying out.
Cash on hand and debts owed
At the end of the campaign's latest report. The date shown is when that report ends.
Where the money came from
Raised, split into individuals giving $200 or less (which campaigns don't have to itemize), individuals giving more, PACs and other committees, party committees, the candidate (gifts and loans), transfers from joint fundraising and other campaign committees, and "other receipts": what's left, such as other loans, refunds and interest.
Donations by size
Individuals' donations, each by its own amount: $200 and under, $200.01–$499.99, $500–$999.99, $1,000–$1,999.99, and $2,000 and over. The smallest group includes the unitemized money.
Where donors live
Itemized donations from individuals, by the state in the donor's address: the candidate's own state, the next three, and all the others together. Unitemized money has no address, so it isn't in this one.
Top donors' employers
Individuals' itemized donations, added up by the employer each donor wrote down. An individual can give a federal candidate only a few thousand dollars, so employers say more than a list of donors would. The eight largest are shown, leaving out answers that name no employer ("Retired", "Self-employed", "None" and the like). These are employees' own donations, not the employers', and they cover only the current two-year period, even for the Senate.
Outside spending
Independent expenditures: money groups spent on the race without working with the campaign, such as super PAC ads, as the groups reported it to the FEC. None of it went to the campaign. Each is marked with a blue "for" or an amber "against" the candidate; the ten largest spenders are shown.

How fresh it is

  • VoteBot keeps the FEC's answers for 7 days, then asks again. Refresh on the FEC in Settings asks right away.
  • A report shows up in the FEC's API once the FEC has processed it, so the newest one may not be counted yet. The "reports through" date says which report the figures reach.

What it doesn't catch

  • Donors who give $200 or less in total don't have to be itemized, so there's no name, employer or state for that money.
  • Employers are added up as donors wrote them: "Dell", "Dell Inc." and "Dell Technologies" count as three employers.
  • Outside spending covers only what groups reported to the FEC as independent expenditures.
How are the Texas Ethics Commission figures put together?

The Texas Ethics Commission publishes every campaign finance report filed with it as one download (about 1 GB), updated nightly. VoteBot boils it down to a much smaller summary, which comes with VoteBot and is rebuilt only when you press Refresh on the Texas Ethics Commission in Settings. Settings shows the date of the summary and the date its money starts from.

Which candidates

  • Candidates and officeholders, judges included. PACs and parties are left out: their own money isn't counted, only what they give to candidates.
  • VoteBot matches them to your ballot by name, with the office and district they told the Texas Ethics Commission they're running for or hold to confirm it. The short name a filer goes by counts too, so "Bob" finds a Robert.

Which reports

  • Counted: candidates' and officeholders' regular reports (such as the semiannual ones, those due 30 and 8 days before an election, and runoff reports) and final reports, for periods ending after the last even-year November general election. For races in 2025–26, that's from November 6, 2024 on; the first report can reach back a few months before that.
  • When a report was corrected, only the corrected version counts.
  • Left out: special pre-election reports (filed daily just before an election) and special-session reports, because the same money is reported again on the next regular report; and reports that aren't about raising money for the office, such as those on leftover contributions or a race for Speaker of the House.

What each figure is

Raised
The total contributions on those reports' cover sheets, small unitemized donations included.
Spent
The total expenditures on the same reports.
Cash on hand and outstanding loans
From the latest report. The date shown is when that report ends.
Where the money came from
Raised, split into small donations that don't have to be itemized, itemized donations from individuals, itemized donations from PACs, businesses and other groups, and whatever is left ("other contributions").
Itemized donations by size
Each itemized donation by its own amount: under $500, $500 to $4,999, $5,000 to $24,999, $25,000 to $99,999, and $100,000 and over.
Where donors live
Itemized donations by the state in the donor's address: Texas, other states, or no address given.
Largest donors
The ten largest itemized donors, their donations added up by name and state and shown as written on their latest donation, with the city and the employer or occupation they gave.
Outside spending naming this candidate
Direct campaign expenditures that others reported making in this candidate's race since the same date, added up by spender; the ten largest are shown. Spending first reported on a daily pre-election report is left out, because it's reported again later.

What it doesn't catch

  • Texas has no contribution limits for most state offices, so one donor can give a lot; judicial races do have limits.
  • Donors are added up by name as written: "Bob Smith" and "Robert Smith" in the same state are two donors, and two different people with the same name in the same state are one.
  • The Texas Ethics Commission doesn't record whether outside spending supported or opposed the candidate.
  • The summary is only as new as its date in Settings. Reports filed since then appear after a Refresh.
  • Officeholders' personal financial statements aren't online. Each candidate's TEC tab links to how to request one.
How do I compare the candidates in a race?

Press "Compare candidates" on a race's money box. It puts everyone in the race side by side: their totals, then each breakdown from their details with one bar per candidate under every category, and their largest donors and outside spenders in columns.

"Dollars" draws each bar against the largest amount in its section. "Share of their money" draws it as a part of that candidate's own money, so a small campaign's mix can be compared with a big one's. A name marked ⇄ is in more than one candidate's list; point at it to see it in the other columns. Names are matched as written in the reports, so the same donor spelled two ways isn't caught, and only each candidate's largest donors are compared.

What is "outside spending"?

Money spent on a race by groups the campaign doesn't control, such as super PACs buying ads. It isn't given to the campaign: the groups spend it themselves. For Congress, the FEC records whether each group spent for or against the candidate, and VoteBot marks it with a blue "for" or an amber "against". The Texas Ethics Commission records which candidate the spending was about, but not whether it was for or against them.

Why doesn't a candidate show any money?

A race's money box lists every candidate on your ballot, and "not found in FEC data" or "not found in TEC data" means VoteBot found no reports for them. Usually that's because:

  • they haven't filed a report yet, or haven't raised or spent enough to have to (a congressional campaign starts reporting to the FEC after $5,000);
  • their name is written differently in the reports than on the ballot, and VoteBot leaves out a match it isn't sure of;
  • for state races, the Texas Ethics Commission summary is older than their first report: press Refresh on it in Settings.

The "Open FEC" or "Open TEC" link under the money box goes to the source's own search, where you can try other spellings.

Why don't county, city or school candidates show any money?

They report to their county or city, not the state (county court at law and probate judges included), and those reports aren't in a form VoteBot can read yet. Your county clerk's or city secretary's website usually has them.

Why does the FEC tab show only totals?

VoteBot is using the FEC's shared demonstration key, which allows only a few requests an hour. Whoever runs VoteBot can get a free key from the OpenFEC developers page, set it as VOTEBOT_FEC_API_KEY (in the environment or VoteBot's .env file), and restart VoteBot. Settings says which key is in use.

Where do the poll numbers come from?

From public polls collected by FiftyPlusOne, for the U.S. Senate, U.S. House and Governor races. They are polls, not a forecast or a chance of winning.

  • Only questions that pit the candidates on your ballot against each other count. An early poll of a matchup that didn't happen (another nominee) is left out.
  • Each pollster counts once, with its latest poll, so a firm that polls every week doesn't outweigh the rest. When a poll asked both likely and registered voters, the likely voters' answer counts.
  • Each candidate's number is the median of their share across those polls. The gray rest of the bar is undecided voters and others.
  • Most U.S. House districts have no public polls at all, so their races show no poll bar.
  • The poll lists are fetched at most once a day. A candidate's Polls tab lists every poll used, with a link to it.

Your picks and notes

Where are my picks and notes saved?

Only in this browser. They aren't sent to the VoteBot server or anyone else, and they don't follow you to another browser or device. Privacy has the details.

How do I take my picks to the polls?

Use "Print my picks" at the top of your ballot. A full page can include your notes, and blank lines for races you haven't picked. A wallet card is a small card, about 3.5 inches wide, to cut out along its dashed edge and fold; it leaves notes out. Both start with Election Day and the early-voting dates. To keep a copy on your computer, choose "Save as PDF" as the printer.

Bring it on paper: phones can't be used at the voting station in Texas.

Where did my picks go?

Picks are kept for each election separately, so choosing another election shows that election's picks. They're also gone if your browser's data for this site was cleared: private windows forget everything when they close, and some browsers delete a site's data after a while without a visit.

Print or save a PDF of your picks if you want a copy that lasts.

How do I delete my picks and my address?

In Settings, "Clear my picks & notes" deletes your picks, notes and write-ins from this browser, and "Clear all browser data" also deletes your remembered address, appearance, search engine and view choices. "Clear picks" at the top of your ballot clears just that election's picks and notes, and "Clear" in a race's heading takes back that race's pick. All of them offer Undo for a few seconds, in case you didn't mean it.

The VoteBot server also keeps a copy of address lookups and suggestions. Privacy explains how to clear it.

Running VoteBot

Can I share my VoteBot with other people?

VoteBot is made to run on your own computer, for you. Settings has no login, so keep it on its default address (127.0.0.1), which only your computer can reach. Its buttons only work from VoteBot's own pages, so another website open in your browser can't use them. Ballotpedia's ballot and address search are unofficial endpoints meant for personal use: turn off "Ballotpedia" and "Address suggestions" before you share VoteBot with anyone.

Will it cover other states?

Not yet. The ballot order comes from the Texas Secretary of State, so each new state needs its own source for it.
