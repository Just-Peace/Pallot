About Pallot

Your vote is yours. Pallot is a personal helper for working through your ballot: its races, what several sources say about each candidate, and a printout of your picks.

What it does

  • Shows the races on your ballot, from your home address or from your voter registration card, anywhere in the 50 states and DC. How much it has depends on your state (see where it covers).
  • Puts what each source says about a candidate side by side. Each candidate shows how much money they've raised and from whom, what the state's filings, Wikidata, TrackAIPAC and Vote for Peace say, and where they stand in public polls, plus a web search. A member of Congress running again shows their newest bills from Congress.gov. A race says when its seat is open, and which party holds it now.
  • Shows when to vote and your districts, with a map.
  • Lets you pick, take notes, and print your picks to take to the polls.

Where it covers

  • Full ballot: Texas. Every federal, state, district, county and precinct race, in ballot order, from the Texas Secretary of State, with your precincts, the key dates and a map of your districts.
  • The state's official list: Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, California, Colorado, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, Wisconsin and Wyoming. The races on the candidate list the state's election office publishes for the November ballot: Congress, statewide offices, the Legislature and some courts, and in Georgia, Nevada, North Carolina and Wisconsin county and city races too, in Indiana its county offices and courts, in Delaware its county offices, and in Vermont its county offices and judges (not its justices of the peace). Maryland's has no county races or circuit court judges. Missouri's has no county races, and your U.S. House district there is on its 2022 map, which the courts kept for 2026. South Carolina's also has its solicitors and the county offices elected countywide (sheriff, probate judge, auditor and the like), but not county council or school board races. Kentucky's has its courts and Commonwealth's Attorneys, but not its county races or the constitutional amendments, and its Governor's race and the other statewide offices in an odd year. Oklahoma's has only the races with more than one candidate, as its ballot does (a candidate with no opponent is elected without a vote), with its district judges and some county offices, but not the judges standing for retention or the state questions. Kansas's has its State Board of Education, placed by your State Senate district, its judges standing for retention and its district judges, but not its district magistrate judges or its county races. Montana's has its Public Service Commission, placed by your State House district, and its Supreme Court, but not its district judges or its county races. South Dakota's has its Public Utilities Commission and its other statewide offices, but not its county races. Hawaii's has its Office of Hawaiian Affairs trustees, elected statewide, and only the races still contested after the primary (a candidate unopposed then is elected), but not its county races. North Dakota's has its statewide offices, but not its Public Service Commission (two races its list doesn't tell apart), its judges or its county races. Mississippi's has Congress, its Court of Appeals and its special elections in 2026, but not its chancery and circuit judges, its county races or its Supreme Court, whose race a federal court has stopped. Minnesota's has Congress, its statewide offices and both chambers of its Legislature, but not its judges or its county races. Arkansas's has Congress, its statewide offices and its Legislature, but not the runoffs for circuit judge and prosecuting attorney or its county races. New Hampshire's has Governor, Congress and its Legislature, a floterial House district's race on the ballot of every district it's made of, but not its Executive Council or its county races. Tennessee's has Governor, Congress and its Legislature, but not its county races or the constitutional amendments. Massachusetts's has Congress, its Governor and Lieutenant Governor as one ticket, its other statewide offices and its Legislature, but not its Governor's Council, its district attorneys or its county races. Louisiana's has Congress and any special election for its Legislature, its U.S. House races an open primary where candidates of every party run together (the top two meet again in December if no one wins a majority), but not its Public Service Commission, BESE, its judges, district attorneys or its parish and city races. Maine's list has only Congress, Governor and the Legislature. New Jersey's has Congress in an even year, and the State Senate and General Assembly (not Governor) in an odd year. Virginia's has Congress in an even year, with any special election for the General Assembly on the same day, and the General Assembly and the statewide offices in an odd year. Nebraska's Legislature is nonpartisan, so its candidates have no party. Washington's candidates are shown with the party they prefer, as its ballot prints it. All of them also have their key dates.
  • Statewide races: Ohio. Its statewide races from the Secretary of State's sample ballot, the U.S. House race from the FEC, and its key dates.
  • Congress only: every other state, and DC. Your U.S. Senate and U.S. House races, from the people who filed with the FEC for the seat and raised $5,000 or more. The FEC doesn't say who made the November ballot, so each is marked Unverified: someone who lost a primary or dropped out may still be listed.

More states are on the way. Wherever you are, your official sample ballot has every race.

What it isn't

  • Not always right. Pallot puts together what public sources say. A source can be late, incomplete or wrong, and Pallot can match the wrong record. Check your official sample ballot from your county or state elections office before you vote.
  • Not official. It isn't tied to any government, election office, party, campaign or source.
  • Not an endorsement. Pallot doesn't back any candidate, party or cause. It shows what the sources say about everyone on your ballot, including endorsements others make. Who you vote for is your choice alone.
  • Not where you register or vote. Vote.gov points you to your state's election office.
  • No warranty. Pallot is provided as is, without warranty of any kind. Its makers and contributors aren't liable for any decision made with it, or for anything a source got wrong.

Open source

Pallot's code is open for anyone to read, check and reuse. Find it on GitHub. How a lookup works is in the FAQ, and what is kept or sent is in Privacy. There are no accounts, ads or tracking. We don't sell your data or keep anything that says who you are, and your picks never leave your browser: we never see your choices.

What's planned, and feedback

Found a problem or have feedback? Report it, see what we're working on, and tell us what you'd like next in the GitHub issues.

Who makes it

Pallot is made by Just-Peace for voters who want to see their whole ballot and weigh every candidate for themselves. We don't back any candidate or party. Your vote is yours.
